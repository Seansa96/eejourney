# 1.4 Structure of the Atom

[[John Dalton]] proposed that all matter was composed of atoms, and the assumptions by him were used to discover the electron, which was done by [[J.J. Thomson]]. Thomson determined that electrons have a negative charge and have little mass relative to the atomic body.  His visualization of the atom was that of the plum pudding model.

In 1913, a Danish scientist named [[Niels Bohr]] presented the most widely accepted theory concerning the structure of an atom. In the Bohr model, electrons exist in specific or "allowed" orbits around the nucleus in much the same way that planets orbit the sun.

The orbit in which the electrons exist is determined by the electron's mass times its speed times the radius of the orbit. These must equal the positive force of the nucleus.

$$
pE=E_{M}*E_{S}*r
$$
When an electron receives enough energy from some other source, it quantum jumps into a higher allowed orbit.  Electrons like to return to a lower orbit, however. When this occurs, the electron emits the excess energy as a singular photon of electromagnetic energy. This movement of electrons is the basis of electric current flow.

# 1.5 Electron Orbits

Each electron orbit of an atom contains a set number of electrons. The number of electrons that can be contained in any one orbit, or shell, is found by the formula
$$
2N^2
$$
where $N$ is the number of the orbit or shell. The first orbit can thus only hold

$$
2(1)^2=2
$$
2 electrons.

The second, third, fourth, and fifth orbits are then thus:


| Orbit | Max Number of Electrons |
| ----- | ----------------------- |
| 1     | 2 Electrons             |
| 2     | 8 Electrons             |
| 3     | 18 Electrons            |
| 4     | 32 Electrons            |
| 5     | 32 Electrons            |

You may note that according to the equation, the fifth shell should hold a theoretical maximum of 50 electrons, but this is rarely the case in known elements, so it is assumed for the vast majority of cases that 32 is the limit.


# 1.6 Valence Electrons

The outer shell of an atom is known as the **valence shell**. Any electrons located in the valence shell are known then as **valence electrons**.  This shell cannot hold more than 8 electrons. Atoms thus with 8 electrons in this shell are then very stable, as they do not readily react with other elements since they have no need to fill this outer shell.

The elements that have 8 valence electrons are called the noble gases, and are inert. These are Neon, Argon, Krypton, Xenon, and Radon.

Atoms with less than 8 electrons in the valence shell can join with other atoms to share valence electrons to form stable molecules or compounds when the shared valence electrons would add up to the desired 8 outer electrons.

Atoms with less than 8 electrons are of course not as stable, and external energy can cause them to escape the attraction of their nucleus. When an electron escapes in this way, it becomes a free electron and the atom is left with a net positive charge.  This is called **[[ionization]]**, and the resulting positive atom is called a positive ion.  

Similarly, this free electron may come to orbit around a neutral atom, causing an imbalance and thus a net negative charge, creating a negative ion.

This is why materials known as **conductors** are known to typically have between one to three valence electrons. Because they are so unstable, the electrons can very easily through external applied charge be made to leave their nucleus and contribute to the flow.

[[Silver]], [[Copper]], and [[Gold]] are all conductors that contain one valence electron and thus are excellent conductors of electricity. Silver is the best natural conductor, followed by copper, gold, and [[Aluminum]]. It is not known however why some materials are better conductors than others. Aluminum, which contains three valence electrons, conducts electricity more readily than [[Platinum]], which only has one valence electron.

# 1.7 The Bump Theory

This theory states that electrons are made to flow through a conductor by the act of electrons from one atom knocking another electron still in orbit around its atom out of orbit.  The striking electron gives its energy to the electron being struck. The striking electron thus may settle into the struck electron's atom, taking its place in its orbit. This creates a sort of pinball chain effect that can be said to be the basis for current.

The energy propagated is divided amongst the amount of valence electrons.  If a free electron strikes an atom containing two valence electrons, the free electron's energy is divided equally amongst the two valence electrons, up to the maximum of 8 valence electrons.


# 1.8 Power Sources

Electric power sources produce a positive and negative terminal, which are each electrodes.  A positive terminal is created by removing electrons, and a negative terminal by creating a surplus. This difference in electric potential creates a pressure that causes the electrons to want to move toward the positive terminal.

# 1.9 Insulators

Materials containing seven or eight valence electrons are subsequently known as **Insulators**.  This is for the reasons stated above. Being stable, and being imparted with far less energy when struck by a moving electron, it is difficult for them to readily conduct current and contribute to the propagation of the flow of electrons. This is why they resist the flow of electricity.

Good examples of insulator are rubber, plastic, glass, and wood.


# 1.10 Semi-conductors

[[Semiconductor|Semiconductors]] are materials that are neither good conductors nor good insulators.  They contain four valence electrons and are characterized by the fact that, as they are heated, their resistance decreases. This is opposed to conductors, which when heated, their resistance increases.

All solid state devices such as diodes, transistors, and integrated circuits are made from combinations of semiconductor materials. 

The two most common materials used in the production of electronic components are [[silicon]] and [[germanium]]. Of the two, silicon is used more often because of its ability to withstand heat, and thus not as quickly lose its resistance, making it more predictable as a material.  To be used in the construction of an electronic device, they must often be [[Doping|doped]] to be either in a positive or negative state.

# 1.12 Methods of Producing Electricity

There are six basic methods for producing electricity:

1. Magnetism
2. Chemical Action
3. Pressure
4. Heat
5. Friction
6. Light

Magnetism is the most common of these methods in the production of electricity. Electromagnetic induction is the operating principle of all generators and alternators.  

The second most common is Chemical Action, which is done by the movement of entire ions instead of just electrons.

The production of electricity by pressure is done by striking, bending, or twisting certain crystals, referred to as piezoelectricity.

Producing electricity with heat is referred to as the Seebeck effect, the primary principle of the Thermocouple.

Static charges are the operating principle of static electricity, which is when two materials are rubbed together to produce friction, transferring electrons from one object to the other.

Producing electricity from light involves the use of particles called photons.  Photons are produced when electrons jump to a lower energy level. This is how gas-filled lights such as sodium vapor, mercury vapor, and so on work.  Electricity can also be produced by photons when they strike a semiconductor material.  The energy of the photon is given to the electron and it is knocked out of orbit in accordance with the Bump theory. This is the operating principle of photovoltaic cells. Other devices that work in similar fashion are photoemissive and photoconductive. These include photodiodes, phototransistors, photo-silicon-controlled rectifiers, and so on. These are typically used when speed is imperative. Photoconductive devices change resistance with a change of light. The most common photoconductive device is the cad cell. These exhibit a resistance of $50 \ohm$ in direct sunlight and several hundred thousand Ohms in complete darkness.


# 1.13 Electrical Effects


Electricity can be used to cause the same effects that produce it, with the exception of friction. Anytime current flows through a conductor, a magnetic field is created around the conductor.

Electricity can also be used to produce certain chemical reactions, like in electroplating, which is done by placing a base metal and a pure metal in a chemical solution.  Since ions move in the solution, and not just electrons, the material to be plated is placed on the negative electrode, while a material like copper is placed on the positive electrode. An applied current causes these ions to travel through solution and deposit on the material to be plated. This is also the basis of electrolysis.

Just like how twisting or bending crystals produces electricity, applying voltage to certain crystals can cause them to oscillate at specific frequencies. This is how radios and crystal earphones work, as well as some clocks.

As mentioned before, heat is often emitted when electrons enter a valence orbit, which is why conductors become warm as current flows through them.  This is how heat-producing devices such as electric ranges, irons, heaters, and so forth operate.

Light is produced when electrons release a photon through jumping to lower orbits.  When electric current is applied to certain conductors, they not only radiate heat, but also photons of light. These are how incandescent lamps work. Some semiconductors can produce light without the heat by-product, such as in the case of light-emitting diodes.
