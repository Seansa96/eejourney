Conduction in metals or typical substrates is commonly thought of as needing to be done through the movement of electrons.  However, in gasses and liquids, this is typically carried out through the movement of ions.

*What are ions?*

Well, ions are just atoms that have either gained or lost electrons.  An atom that has lost electrons is said to be positively charged, while gaining electrons results in a negative ion.

Batteries, for example, require the movement of ions through liquids in order to work, while fluorescent lights rely on the movement of ions through gasses.

# The Ionization Process 

We'll demonstrate this with a common metallic salt known as [[Magnesium Chloride]].  By its name, it can be inferred of course that this is a compound of [[Magnesium]] and [[Chlorine]].  Magnesium has two valence electrons and is a metal, while Chlorine has seven and is a nonmetal.

Through heating the Magnesium in the presence of Chlorine gas, a Magnesium atom may combine with two atoms of Chlorine to form a [[metallic salt]], which is the Magnesium Chloride aforementioned.  

During this process, the Magnesium gives up its two valence electrons to the two Chlorine atoms.  This is because each Chlorine atom has seven in its outer shell and only needs 1 to be stable.  Once this happens, the Magnesium atom becomes a positively charged ion while the Chlorine atoms become negative.

This is similar in formation to table salt, which is similarly formed by combining a metal, Sodium, with Chlorine.  When metallic salts are mixed into a non-conducting liquid such as distilled water, the water becomes conductive as it now has ions that can carry an electric current.

A simple example of such is a simple circuit made with copper electrodes.  One electrode is connected to a 120V source, and the other to a load, a simple lamp, and are themselves submerged into a glass of distilled water.  When power is switched on to the circuit, the lamp does not glow. As salt is added to the water, however, the lamp begins to brighten as ions begin to realize the voltage difference.

*What happens as the solution becomes concentrated with the salt?* The lamp continues to brighten until its limiting factor becomes the resistance inherent to the load, this being the filament.

Other compounds can be used however for this purpose. Acids and alkalis also function well. The solutions created by adding salts, acids, or alkalis to a liquid are referred to as electrolytes.

# 11.2 Other Types of Ions

11.1 discussed ions formed with single atoms, however, it is possible to form ions with multiple atoms, or molecules, as well.

One such useful compound is [[Sulfuric Acid]]. It's an electrolyte that is commonly used in lead-acid batteries, like those used in cars.  Its chemical formula, $H_2 SO_4$ indicates that one molecule of Sulfuric Acid contains 2 Hydrogen, 1 Sulfur, and 4 Oxygen atoms.

However, Sulfuric Acid in its pure form is not useful as an electrolyte. Combined with water however, the molecules separate into ions of positive Hydrogen and negative Sulfate ($SO_4$).  The two electrons that the Hydrogen ions donated to the Sulfate are actually responsible for holding the Sulfate together in its outer (valence) shell.

Another useful compound is [[Copper Sulfate]], $CuSO_4$. In its natural form, it appears as a metallic salt with blue crystals.  When it is mixed with water, the [[Copper]] atoms separates into a positive Copper ion and a negative Sulfate ion.  The copper ion is called a Cupric ion as its lost two electrons, and the Sulfate ion is similar to when the Sulfuric acid mixed with water and become negatively charged.

Copper Sulfate is typically used in Electroplating.

# 11.3 Electroplating

Electroplating is the process of depositing atoms from one type of metal on to another.

These are the conditions to successfully conduct this process:

* The electrolyte solution has to contain ions of the metal to be plated.
* The metal ions are positively charged.
* The object to be plated has to be connected to the negative terminal (cathode).
* Direct current is used as the power source.
* The positive terminal (anode) is connected to the metal that is to be deposited, and it also must be made of the same metal that is to be the coating.

An example of electroplating is that involving copper and cuprous cyanide.  The copper is submerged into the cuprous cyanide solution and connected to the anode, while the object to be plated is connected to the cathode.

When the circuit is switched on, the positive copper ions (which are not cupric, since they only lose one electron) move through the solution towards the metal, and the solution separates the cyanide atoms into negative cyanide ions, which replenish the charge of the copper bar.  

As the copper ions accumulate on the metal to be plated, they receive electrons from it and become neutral, settling on the surface of the metal and forming a layer of copper. The thickness of this coating is based on the length of time this process is continued for.

This process is actually quite common for purifying copper. Impure copper actually has quite high resistance and is not suitable for use as a conductor, so wire manufacturers typically require electrolytically purified copper.

The reason this process is useful for purification is because when you connect raw/impure copper to the anode and pure copper to the cathode, only pure copper ions will leave the anode, leaving only impurities either at the anode or in solution.

# 11.4 Electrolysis

What was done to the copper in the previous section is actually an example of electrolysis, which is the chemical decomposition of a compound through electrical current. This is a proven and popular way to separate elements into their pure form, which is often necessary since they are rarely found without impurities or alone.

[[Aluminum]] is such an element that often requires electrolysis. It is often found as [[ALuminum Oxide]] in the Earth's crust quite abundantly, while pure Aluminum is quite scarce.  It used to be very expensive and difficult to separate them, but now it is simple enough to do so.  In this way, the oxygen atoms can be separated into positive ions, donating their electrons to the positive Aluminum ions, turning them back into neutral, Aluminum atoms.

# 11.5 Conduction in Gasses

Typically, air is an excellent insulator at atmospheric pressure and it is actually quite hard to use it to carry electric current, often requiring an immense difference in voltage potential to overcome the resistance.

When this voltage threshold is reached, then the resulting current manifests in an arc, which is a visible, luminous discharge of electricity through a gas.

The voltage required to overcome an air gap you may be unsurprised to learn depends on the distance between the two conductors.  However, once an arc has been established, the air in between becomes ionized and the voltage required is reduced, which is why circuits involving air gaps are typically variable voltage in the power supply.

The amount of voltage required for an electron to cause ionization is called the ionization potential.

These are the factors that help determine what that value is:

* Atmospheric pressure, because increased pressure results in a constricted mean free path for the electrons, causing them to collide with gas atoms more often, reducing their average kinetic energy. When pressure is reduced, their mean path becomes longer, collisions are reduced, and they on average have much higher kinetic energy to knock electrons off of other atoms.
* The type of gas. Different gasses require different voltages. Sodium vapor requires 5 for example, while Mercury requires 10.4 volts.

## Electron Impact

The most important factor in conduction in gasses however is that of the electron impact, which is just a technical term for an electron colliding with another electron or object.

If an electron has sufficient energy, which is proportional to the applied voltage and the distance it travels, it will knock off an electron from a gas molecule, which in turn that electron will propagate forward carrying out the same action, cascading the effect further and ionizing the substrate.

If it does not have enough potential however, it simply bounces off the gas molecule and returns to the positive terminal before "trying" again.

## Useful Applications

One such use of conduction in gasses is the X-ray, invented by Wilhelm Roentgen.  He discovered that when cathode rays struck metal or glass, a new kind of radiation was produced.  These passed through most materials without effect, and incidentally passed through flesh easier than bone, allowing the projections of the bones to be captured and displayed.
