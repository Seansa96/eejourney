
# Series Resistances

![[Pasted image 20260130003823.png]]

In a **series** circuit, all resistors experience the current equally.

![[Pasted image 20260130003958.png]]

The voltage across each resister is $V(x)$, where $x$ is the resistor, equal to $I \times R(x)$. Thus, the **series voltage** is the summation of each $V(x)$ in the circuit.

Likewise, the resistance of the series resistance is the summation of each resistor. So,

$$
R(total)=Ra+Rb+Rc
$$
And as such can be represented by a single resistor.

# Voltage Divider

![[Pasted image 20260130005020.png]]

By chaining a series resistor, we can change the output voltage.  The above arrangement is referred to as a **voltage divider**. 

The value of the output voltage, $Vout$, is the ratio of resistors $Ra$ and $Rb$.

$$
Vout = Vin \times \frac{Rb}{Ra+Rb}
$$

# Parallel Resistors

Here are resistors connected in parallel:
![[Pasted image 20260130005838.png]]

The voltages here are 

![[Pasted image 20260130010032.png]]

Thus, we can see that the voltages would then be the same across each resistor, as $Vin = Va = Vb = Vc$.

Then, according to **Kirchoff's** law, we know the total current entering the current should be the same leaving it, so $Itotal = Ia+Ib+Ic$. 

However, when we consider Ohm's law, we know that since each resistor has a different resistance value, it will have a different value of current across it, since $I = VR$. While they all have the same voltage across them, the resistance can be different.

We still can represent a chain of resistors in a parallel circuit by a single equivalent resistor, but there are some changes from a series resistors.

![[Pasted image 20260130010709.png]]

The formula instead of a simple summation, is itself a quotient of the combined quotients of the resistors.

$$
\frac{1}{Rtotal} = \frac{1}{Ra} + \frac{1}{Rb} + \frac{1}{Rc}
$$

For $n$ amount of resistors.

Note that the parallel resistor equivalent total will be less than each individual resistor.

# Impedance - Resistive Loads

![[Pasted image 20260130012147.png]]

In a circuit that contains a purely resistive load, the power factor is just 1 (since $cos\theta$ is 1), and the current and voltage waveforms will be in phase as there is no difference in their phase angle, by definition it will be 0.

This is easy to see when looking at a Phasor Diagram.
