
The study of **Physics** can be divided into six main areas:

1. Classical Mechanics, concerning the motion of objects that are large relative to atoms and move at speeds much slower than the speed of light.
2. Relativity, a theory describing objects moving at any speed, even those approaching the speed of light.
3. Thermodynamics, dealing with heat, temperature, and the statistical behavior of systems with large numbers of particles.
4. Electromagnetism, concerning electricity, magnetism, and electro-magnetic fields.
5. Optics, the study of the behavior of light and its interactions with materials.
6. Quantum mechanics, a collection of theories connecting the behavior of matter at the submicroscopic level to macroscopic observations.


Classical mechanics is also referred to as Newtonian Mechanics, or simply mechanics.  

# The Particle Model

The particle model is a useful model when analyzing scenarios in which size is determined to have no consequence on the outcome or solution.

For example, when modeling the gravitational force between the Earth and Sun, only their masses and distance between centers matters. Thus, it is convenient and perfectly agreeable to assume that the Earth can be represented as a particle of zero size but with the same mass.

## Simplification Model

The next model is known as the **Simplification Model**, which is what the particle model was. In a simplification model, details that are not significant in determining the outcome of the problem are ignored.

## Analysis Model


The third category is that of **analysis models**, which are general types of problems that we have solved before. There are around two dozen of these analysis models, based on four of the simplification models, which are: particle, system, rigid object, and wave.
## Structural Model


The fourth category of models is structural models. These are used to understand the behavior of a  system of far different scale from the macroscopic world, in either direction.

# Dimensional Analysis

In Physics, **dimension** denotes the physical nature of a quantity. The distance between two points can be expressed in terms of the dimension of length.

$L, M, T$ are the symbols used for length, mass, and time respectively in this book. Brackets, or \[] will often be used to denote the dimensions of a physical quantity, for example for speed, the dimensions will be written as:

$$
[v]=\frac{L}{T}
$$
or area,

$$
[A]=L^2
$$

Dimensional Analysis is just another way of formalizing algebraic expressions and manipulation and ensuring that the dimensions of a given or derived equation ends with both sides resulting in equal units.

As an example, 

Suppose  you  are  interested  in  an  equation  for  the  position $x$ of a car at a time $t$ if the car starts from rest at $x=0$ and moves with constant acceleration $a$. The correct expression for this situation is $x=\frac{1}{2}at^2$as we show in Chapter 2. The quantity $x$ on the left side has the dimension of length. For the equation to be dimensionally correct, the quantity on the right side must also have the dimension of length. 

Substituting in the dimensions, we get:
$$
L=\frac{L}{T^2}*T^2
$$

As you can see, the $T$ dimension cancels out, resulting in the $L$ dimension on both sides.

Another way to set this up is by using the notation
$$
x \propto a^nt^m
$$
The exponents here must be determined, and then the relationship will only be correct if the dimensions of both sides are the same.

For the above, we have 

$$
[a^nt^m]=L=L^1T^0
$$

We can see here that the $T$ should cancel out to $1$, leaving just $L^1$ which is just $L$.
