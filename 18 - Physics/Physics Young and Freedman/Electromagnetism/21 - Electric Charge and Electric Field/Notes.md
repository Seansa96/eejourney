The protons and neutrons are held by a different force, called the strong nuclear force (or just strong force).  This overcomes the repulsion of the protons.

The strong nuclear force has a short range however, and does not extend beyond the nucleus.

## Electric Charge is Conserved

The principle of conservation of charge is defined thusly: The algebraic sum of all the electric charges in any closed system is constant.

In any charging process then, no charge is created or destroyed, it is merely transferred from one body to another. This is thought to be a universal conservation law. There is no experimental evidence for any violation of this principle.  Even when particles are created or destroyed in high-energy interactions like annihilation, the total charge of the closed system is constant (like with electrons and positrons).

Every amount of electric charge is always an integer multiple of that fundamental charge, and so thus charge is quantized or said to be quantized.  You cannot divide that amount of charge any further. So, the charge of any macroscopic body is always 0 or some multiple of this charge.

# Conductors, Insulators, and Induced Charges

Some materials permit electric charge to move easily from one region of material to another, while other don't. 
![[Pasted image 20261026174233.png]]

A copper wire is an example of such a material, called a **conductor**. These permit easy movement of charge through them, while **insulators** do not.

More specifically, in conductors, electrons at the outershells, or valence electrons, occupy more delocalized states, not directly attached to the atoms and instead located throughout the lattice. This gives them relative freedom of mobility in response to an applied electric field.

In contrast, insulators have large band gaps between the filled valence bad and the empty conduction band. This means that to move across that conduction band, they require a large amount of energy to enter a conducting state .  

In an example case of triboelectric charge transfer, rubbing your shoes against carpet fibers, which are good insulators, causes charge to build up on you because it cannot flow through the insulating fibers, so it cannot freely discharge through potential difference driven current.

If you then touch a conducting object like a doorknob, a rapid charge transfer takes place between your finger and the doorknob, and you feel a shock. You can prevent this by winding some carpet fibers around conducting cores so that any charge that builds up on you can be transferred to the carpet harmlessly.

You could also coat carpet fibers with an anti-static layer that doesn't easily transfer electrons between you and your shoes.

## Charging by Induction

We can provide charge to other bodies of opposite signs without losing any of our own charge through a process called charging by **electrostatic induction**.  By bringing a negatively charged or positively charged near an uncharged object, like a metal ball, without touching it, the free electrons in the metal ball are repelled by the excess electrons on the rod, causing them to shift opposite of the rod. This creates a charge distribution, and is said to **polarize** the object--creating a separation of charge.  Because they cannot escape from the ball because the connecting material are insulators, they remain in this distribution, and in this way the charges are said to be **induced**.

It is not quite all of the free electrons that move to the right surface of the ball though. As soon as any induced charge develops, it exerts forced toward the *left* on the other free electrons.  These electrons are repelled by the negative induced charge on the right and attracted toward the positive induced charge on the left.  As soon as there is any induced chare, then it would exert forces toward the *left* on the other free electrons. These are then repelled by the negative induced charge on the right and attracted to the positive induced charge on the left. 

This results in an equilibrium state in which the force toward the right is balanced by the force on the left--the induced positive charge balances the negatively charged rod's force.  

But what happens when we touch  we touch one end of a conducting wire wire while the rod is still nearby? The conducting wire serves as a path, referred to in this case as **ground**, which allows the electrons a path that they can now exit through, and the grounding path can be said to be a "sink" of these electrons.

## Electric Forces on Uncharged Objects

Note that a charged body can exert forces even on uncharged objects, like we saw with charging by electrostatic induction.

If you rub a balloon on the rug and then hold the balloon against the ceiling, it'll stick even though the ceiling has no net electric charge, or, you can pick up uncharged bits of paper after running a comb through your hair and electrifying it.

This is due to an induced-charge effect. Even in an insulator, electric charges shift back and forth a little when there is charge nearby. Because of the inverse square law, closer objects will exert a greater electric force than distant ones. So if positive and negative charges in a material are present in equal amounts, but the positive charges are closer to a negatively charged object, there will be a net attractive force.

# Coulumb's Law

For **point charges** which are those that are very small in comparison with $r$ or the distance between them, Coulumb found that the electric force is proportional to $1/r^2$ or that when the distance doubles, the force falls to one fourth of its initial value. When it is cut in half, we quadruple the force.

It also depends on the amount of charge $q$ on each body, and the sign of that charge. In exploring this dependency, he divided a charge into two equal parts by placing a small charged spherical conductor into contact with an identical but uncharged sphere. By symmetry, it is observed that the charge is shared equally between them, by the principle of conservation of charge.

Thus, he could obtain any ratio of initial charge. Therefore, the product of the charge is proportional to the forces each charge in the relation exerts on each other.

Succinctly, we can express this with **Coulumb's Law**, which states that:


> [!Textbook]
> The magnitude of the electric force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.
> $$F=k \frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{r^2}
> $$

Here, $k$ is a proportionality constant whose value depends on the system of units used, but typically is equal to

$$
\frac{1}{4\pi \varepsilon_{0}} \tag{Coulumb's Constant}
$$


The directions the forces the two charges exert on each other are always along the line that joins them. When the charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ have the same sign, the forces are repulsive, and when they are different, the forces are attractive, and they obey Newton's Third Law.

# Electric Field and Electric Forces

It is important to note that an isolated charge experiences no net electric force. While it exists in a field and there are said to be radial lines of the electric field it itself produces, that it what a **positive test charge would experience if placed there--NOT THE CHARGE THAT ITSELF IS PRODUCING THAT FIELD**.  What this means is that there NEEDS to be another test charge of any sign for that field projected by that isolated charge to exert a force.

It is also that electric force acts only along the line connecting two charges, and that they are acting on *different objects*. Observe the following:
![[Pasted image 20261013150143.png]]

Because the fields are acting on different charges, they don't sum, so they don't cancel and the forces are applied independently to the charges.

That's the same reason charges can attract. The charges through their electric fields push each other towards each other. Because the magnitudes are the same, they essentially "stop" when they reach each other, but if they are somehow separated again they will just continue to push back along that line towards each other.

Electric field lines show the direction of at each point, and their spacing
gives a general idea of the magnitude of at each point. Where is strong, we
draw lines bunched closely together; where is weaker, they are farther apart.
At any particular point, the electric field has a unique direction, so only one field
line can pass through each point of the field. In other words, field lines never
intersect.

This is related to the fact that the Electric field is an antiparallel gradient field, and thus a field line will point in the direction of decreasing electric potential.
![[Pasted image 20260930152616.png]]


Electric field lines are not the same as trajectories It’s a common misconception that if a charged particle of charge is in motion where there is an electric field, the
particle must move along an electric field line. Because at any point is tangent to the field
line that passes through that point, it is indeed true that the force on the particle, and
hence the particle’s acceleration, are tangent to the field line. But we learned in Chapter 3
that when a particle moves on a curved path, its acceleration cannot be tangent to the path.
So in general, the trajectory of a charged particle is not the same as a field line

## Electric Dipole Forces and Torque

To start with the first question, let’s place an electric dipole in a uniform exter-
nal electric field as shown in Fig. 21.31. The forces $E+$ and $E-$ and on the two
charges both have magnitude $qE$ , but their directions are opposite, and they add
to zero. *The net force on an electric dipole in a uniform external electric field is*
*zero.*

That force however is or can be thought of as parallel between them. The perpendicular force about the center of the dipole is perpendicular, so it is useful to measure this in terms of torque, using a cross product.

Both of the charges experience this torque, and it is in the same direction, thus they sum together.

If the torque can be thought of as 

$$
r = (qE)(d\sin \theta)
$$
where $qE$ is the Electric Force, $d$ is the distance between the charges, and $\sin \theta$ arises from the cross product.

The $d$ in this case represents the sum of each charge's distance $\frac{d}{2}$.

At its maximum the equation can be simplified to

$$
r=qd
$$
since $\sin \theta$ at that point is $sin (1)=1$.

The units of the dipole moment are Coulumbs $*$ meters.

And for the electric field, it's torque on the dipole is:

 $r=pE sin f$ (magnitude of the torque on an electric dipole)

## Potential Energy of an Electric Dipole

When a dipole changes direction in an electric field, the electric-field torque does
work on it, with a corresponding change in potential energy. The work $dW$ done by a torque $r$ during an infinitesimal displacement $d\phi$ is
$$
dW=r d\phi
$$
However, since the $E$ points in the direction of decreasing potential, this causes the Work to also go in that same direction.

$$
dW = - r d \phi
$$
And since the torque is $pEsin(\theta)$.

$$
dW=-pE\sin(\theta)d \phi
$$
Thus, if we have some finite displacement from $\phi1$ to $\phi2$, we can represent this with an integral.

![[Pasted image 20260930161101.png]]

If 

$$
U(\phi)=pE\cos(\phi)
$$
where $U$ is potential energy, then

$$
\Delta U = pE\cos(\phi_{1})-pE\cos(\phi_{2})
$$
and if $W$ is the *negative* change in potential energy, then

$$
W=pE\cos(\phi_{2})-pE\cos(\phi_{1})
$$
