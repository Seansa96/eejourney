## Q21.1: Contact electrification

Explain why two tape strips peeled from one roll repel, but attract after their sticky and shiny sides are pressed together and separated.

**Solution:**
Peeling from the roll can transfer electrons in the same sense to both strips, leaving them with charges of the same sign. Separating the two different contacting surfaces can transfer electrons from one strip to the other, leaving opposite signs and hence attraction. The observation does not establish which strip is positive.

**Why it works:**
Like charges repel and unlike charges attract; electrons transfer while total charge is conserved.

**Grading key points:**

- Peeling from the roll can transfer electrons in the same sense to both strips, leaving them with charges of the same sign.
- Separating the two different contacting surfaces can transfer electrons from one strip to the other, leaving opposite signs and hence attraction.
- The observation does not establish which strip is positive.

## Q21.2: Conductor polarization and charge conservation

Two isolated metal spheres on nylon threads attract. List the possible charge states. Can they remain electrostatically stuck after touching?

**Solution:**
They may have opposite net charges, or one may be neutral and polarized by the charged sphere. Even like net charges can attract at sufficiently close range if unequal charges produce strong polarization; attraction alone is not a definitive sign test. On contact they form one equipotential conductor. After redistribution, ordinary isolated spheres either repel if net charge remains or have no lasting electrostatic attraction if neutralized; other adhesion mechanisms are excluded.

**Why it works:**
Induced surface charge matters as well as net charge. Contact equalizes potential, not necessarily the charges on unequal spheres.

**Grading key points:**

- They may have opposite net charges, or one may be neutral and polarized by the charged sphere.
- Even like net charges can attract at sufficiently close range if unequal charges produce strong polarization; attraction alone is not a definitive sign test.
- On contact they form one equipotential conductor.
- After redistribution, ordinary isolated spheres either repel if net charge remains or have no lasting electrostatic attraction if neutralized; other adhesion mechanisms are excluded.

## Q21.3: Polarization versus field gradient

If electric force magnitudes did not decrease with separation, would a charged comb still polarize a neutral insulator and attract it?

**Solution:**
It could still polarize it: positive and negative bound charges experience opposite forces and shift relative to each other. In the simple aligned-dipole model, however, the near opposite charge and the far like charge experience equal force magnitudes, so their attractions and repulsions cancel. The usual near-side-stronger argument for attraction would disappear.

**Why it works:**
A field creates a dipole; a spatial variation in the field along that dipole creates the usual net translational force.

**Grading key points:**

- It could still polarize it: positive and negative bound charges experience opposite forces and shift relative to each other.
- In the simple aligned-dipole model, however, the near opposite charge and the far like charge experience equal force magnitudes, so their attractions and repulsions cancel.
- The usual near-side-stronger argument for attraction would disappear.

## Q21.4: Triboelectric charging

Why do clothes cling after drying, and how should a load of one material compare with a mixed-material load?

**Solution:**
Rubbing and separating fabrics transfers electrons; opposite charges attract, and charged fabric can also attract neutral fabric by polarization. Dry conditions reduce charge leakage. Identical fabrics generally have less systematic contact charging than dissimilar materials, so an all-cotton load should usually cling less, although surface conditions can still produce charge.

**Why it works:**
Contact charging depends on the contacting materials and moisture, not simply on motion.

**Grading key points:**

- Rubbing and separating fabrics transfers electrons; opposite charges attract, and charged fabric can also attract neutral fabric by polarization.
- Dry conditions reduce charge leakage.
- Identical fabrics generally have less systematic contact charging than dissimilar materials, so an all-cotton load should usually cling less, although surface conditions can still produce charge.

## Q21.5: Induction followed by contact charging

Why does a neutral suspended metal sphere first approach a positive glass rod, then recoil after touching it?

**Solution:**
Initially electrons shift toward the rod, creating a nearby negative surface and a farther positive surface; the nearer attraction dominates. On touching, electrons can leave the sphere for the rod, giving the sphere a positive net charge. It is then repelled by the positively charged rod.

**Why it works:**
Polarization can attract a neutral conductor; charge transfer changes its net charge.

**Grading key points:**

- Initially electrons shift toward the rod, creating a nearby negative surface and a farther positive surface; the nearer attraction dominates.
- On touching, electrons can leave the sphere for the rod, giving the sphere a positive net charge.
- It is then repelled by the positively charged rod.

## Q21.6: Electrostatic equilibrium with gravity

Why do a metal's free electrons not all settle to its bottom under gravity?

**Solution:**
A tiny downward redistribution produces a restoring electric field long before macroscopic settling occurs. In the simplest electron-force model equilibrium requires $-e\mathbf E+m_e\mathbf g=0$, so the required field scale is only $m_eg/e$. Lattice support, compressibility, and electron pressure affect the detailed real-metal distribution.

**Why it works:**
Because electrical forces are so strong, extremely small charge separations can balance gravity; electrons also obey quantum statistics.

**Grading key points:**

- A tiny downward redistribution produces a restoring electric field long before macroscopic settling occurs.
- In the simplest electron-force model equilibrium requires $-e\mathbf E+m_e\mathbf g=0$, so the required field scale is only $m_eg/e$.
- Lattice support, compressibility, and electron pressure affect the detailed real-metal distribution.

## Q21.7: Field superposition and nulls

Three equal-magnitude charges lie at $y=-a,0,+a$ with the field-line arrows shown in Figure Q21.7. Determine their signs and where the finite-plane field magnitude is smallest.

![Field-line arrows for three equal-magnitude charges arranged vertically.](18%20-%20Physics/Physics%20Young%20and%20Freedman/assets/figure-q21-7.png)

**Solution:**
Arrows leave the outer charges and terminate on the center charge: outer charges are positive and the center is negative. The smallest field is zero at two lateral points on the perpendicular bisector. At $(x,0)$ the vertical components cancel and $E_x=kqx[2/(x^2+a^2)^{3/2}-1/|x|^3]$. Thus $x=\pm a/\sqrt{2^{2/3}-1}$, about $\pm1.305a$. In three dimensions these points belong to a circular ring of nulls.

**Why it works:**
The two outer positive charges' lateral field balances the central negative charge's opposite field; line spacing suggests these cancellation regions.

**Grading key points:**

- Arrows leave the outer charges and terminate on the center charge: outer charges are positive and the center is negative.
- The smallest field is zero at two lateral points on the perpendicular bisector.
- At $(x,0)$ the vertical components cancel and $E_x=kqx[2/(x^2+a^2)^{3/2}-1/|x|^3]$.
- Thus $x=\pm a/\sqrt{2^{2/3}-1}$, about $\pm1.305a$.
- In three dimensions these points belong to a circular ring of nulls.

## Q21.8: Mobile carriers and thermal transport

Why are metals usually good conductors of both heat and electricity, while wood is usually poor at both?

**Solution:**
Metals have mobile electrons that transport charge and also carry thermal energy between regions. Wood lacks comparable mobile charge carriers and its structure impedes heat transport. Lattice vibrations also carry heat, so this is a useful correlation rather than a universal rule.

**Why it works:**
The same electron population can contribute to both charge and energy transport; electrical insulation does not universally imply thermal insulation.

**Grading key points:**

- Metals have mobile electrons that transport charge and also carry thermal energy between regions.
- Wood lacks comparable mobile charge carriers and its structure impedes heat transport.
- Lattice vibrations also carry heat, so this is a useful correlation rather than a universal rule.

## Q21.9: Field lines versus trajectories

A positive particle is released from rest near (a) one fixed positive point charge and (b) a fixed electric dipole. Must it follow a field line?

**Solution:**
For the lone positive charge, symmetry makes both force and subsequent velocity radial, so the particle follows a straight radial field line outward. In a dipole field, acceleration is tangent to the instantaneous field but accumulated velocity need not be. Its path generally does not follow a curved field line, although special symmetry-axis trajectories can.

**Why it works:**
Newton's law relates field to acceleration, not directly to velocity.

**Grading key points:**

- For the lone positive charge, symmetry makes both force and subsequent velocity radial, so the particle follows a straight radial field line outward.
- In a dipole field, acceleration is tangent to the instantaneous field but accumulated velocity need not be.
- Its path generally does not follow a curved field line, although special symmetry-axis trajectories can.

## Q21.10: Charging by induction

How can two identical isolated metal objects be given opposite charges of exactly equal magnitude?

**Solution:**
Start with both neutral and touching. Bring a charged rod near one object without contact; electrons redistribute between the objects. Separate the objects while the rod remains nearby, then remove the rod. Their total charge is still zero, so their final net charges are equal and opposite.

**Why it works:**
Induction separates preexisting charges without adding charge to the two-object system.

**Grading key points:**

- Start with both neutral and touching.
- Bring a charged rod near one object without contact; electrons redistribute between the objects.
- Separate the objects while the rod remains nearby, then remove the rod.
- Their total charge is still zero, so their final net charges are equal and opposite.

## Q21.11: Electrostatic adhesion

Why can plastic food wrap adhere to a container? Does it adhere equally to itself, and can it adhere to metal?

**Solution:**
Contact/separation can charge the wrap; its electric field polarizes the container and creates attraction. Two pieces carrying similar charge may repel, while differently charged or polarized pieces can attract, so self-adhesion need not be equal. Metal can adhere because its mobile electrons polarize strongly. Actual cling also depends on surface contact and short-range intermolecular forces.

**Why it works:**
Neutral objects can be attracted by induced charge; conductivity does not prevent polarization.

**Grading key points:**

- Contact/separation can charge the wrap; its electric field polarizes the container and creates attraction.
- Two pieces carrying similar charge may repel, while differently charged or polarized pieces can attract, so self-adhesion need not be equal.
- Metal can adhere because its mobile electrons polarize strongly.
- Actual cling also depends on surface contact and short-range intermolecular forces.

## Q21.12: Humidity and discharge capacity

Why are shocks after walking on nylon more common in dry air, and why is an isolated paper clip less likely to produce a substantial shock than a doorknob?

**Solution:**
Dry surfaces let transferred charge persist; moisture promotes leakage. A large metal object or grounded doorknob can accept more charge while its potential changes little, permitting a larger discharge. A small isolated clip quickly approaches the body's potential and generally accepts less charge. If the clip is grounded, its small size alone does not prevent a shock.

**Why it works:**
Discharge depends on accumulated charge, capacitance, grounding, and leakage, not just the presence of metal.

**Grading key points:**

- Dry surfaces let transferred charge persist; moisture promotes leakage.
- A large metal object or grounded doorknob can accept more charge while its potential changes little, permitting a larger discharge.
- A small isolated clip quickly approaches the body's potential and generally accepts less charge.
- If the clip is grounded, its small size alone does not prevent a shock.

## Q21.13: Contact and induction charging

Using a negative object, how can an insulated metal sphere acquire negative charge or positive charge?

**Solution:**
For negative charging, touch it with the negative object so electrons can transfer, then separate. For positive charging, bring the negative object close without contact, ground the sphere so repelled electrons escape, remove the ground while the object remains, then remove the object.

**Why it works:**
Contact transfers electrons onto the sphere; induction with grounding removes electrons from it.

**Grading key points:**

- For negative charging, touch it with the negative object so electrons can transfer, then separate.
- For positive charging, bring the negative object close without contact, ground the sphere so repelled electrons escape, remove the ground while the object remains, then remove the object.

## Q21.14: Coulomb force and initial acceleration

Two equal charges of equal mass each initially accelerate at $a_0$ when released. What is one charge's initial acceleration if the other is held fixed at the same separation?

**Solution:**
It remains $a_0$. The initial electric force $kq^2/r^2$ and the moving particle's mass have not changed. Later accelerations can differ between the experiments because the separations evolve differently.

**Why it works:**
The initial force depends on the present configuration, not on whether the other particle will move.

**Grading key points:**

## Q21.15: Newton's third law

Equal masses carry $Q$ and $2Q$. If the $Q$ particle initially accelerates with magnitude $a_0$, what is the other particle's initial acceleration?

**Solution:**
Its acceleration also has magnitude $a_0$, in the opposite direction. Each particle experiences the same interaction-force magnitude $k|Q(2Q)|/r^2$, and their masses are equal.

**Why it works:**
Action and reaction forces are equal even when the charges differ.

**Grading key points:**

- Its acceleration also has magnitude $a_0$, in the opposite direction.
- Each particle experiences the same interaction-force magnitude $k|Q(2Q)|/r^2$, and their masses are equal.

## Q21.16: Force versus acceleration

Compare the forces, accelerations, and release directions of a proton and electron placed separately in the same uniform electric field.

**Solution:**
Their forces have equal magnitudes $eE$ and opposite directions. Their accelerations have magnitudes $eE/m_p$ and $eE/m_e$, so the electron's is about 1836 times larger. The proton initially moves with the field and the electron against it.

**Why it works:**
The charge sign determines force direction and mass determines acceleration for a given force.

**Grading key points:**

- Their forces have equal magnitudes $eE$ and opposite directions.
- Their accelerations have magnitudes $eE/m_p$ and $eE/m_e$, so the electron's is about 1836 times larger.
- The proton initially moves with the field and the electron against it.

## Q21.17: Macroscopic electrical neutrality

Why do we feel Earth's gravity but not a comparably enormous electrical force, despite electric interactions between alpha particles being about $10^{35}$ times stronger than gravity?

**Solution:**
Earth and ordinary bodies contain nearly equal positive and negative charges, so most large-scale electric forces cancel. Mass contributions to gravity have the same attractive sign and add. Residual terrestrial electric fields exist but are much weaker than the fields of a comparably large uncompensated charge.

**Why it works:**
Charge is signed and usually balanced; ordinary gravitational mass does not cancel this way.

**Grading key points:**

- Earth and ordinary bodies contain nearly equal positive and negative charges, so most large-scale electric forces cancel.
- Mass contributions to gravity have the same attractive sign and add.
- Residual terrestrial electric fields exist but are much weaker than the fields of a comparably large uncompensated charge.

## Q21.18: Comparison of inverse-square forces

Compare electric and gravitational forces, including their most important differences.

**Solution:**
For stationary point sources both are central inverse-square forces, conservative, long-ranged, and obey superposition. Gravity between ordinary positive masses is attractive; electric forces can attract or repel. Electric forces are far stronger between elementary charged particles and can be screened by rearranging opposite charges. Electric force depends on charge, while gravity couples to mass-energy.

**Why it works:**
The mathematical inverse-square form is shared, but the source properties and signs differ.

**Grading key points:**

- For stationary point sources both are central inverse-square forces, conservative, long-ranged, and obey superposition.
- Gravity between ordinary positive masses is attractive; electric forces can attract or repel.
- Electric forces are far stronger between elementary charged particles and can be screened by rearranging opposite charges.
- Electric force depends on charge, while gravity couples to mass-energy.

## Q21.20: Nuclear binding

Why does the existence of nuclei containing protons and neutrons imply an interaction beyond electric force and gravity?

**Solution:**
Protons repel electrically, while neutron neutrality supplies no compensating electrostatic attraction. Gravity at nuclear scales is far too weak to overcome proton repulsion. The strong nuclear interaction supplies the short-range binding of nucleons.

**Why it works:**
A stable bound nucleus requires an attractive interaction strong enough at femtometer separations.

**Grading key points:**

- Protons repel electrically, while neutron neutrality supplies no compensating electrostatic attraction.
- Gravity at nuclear scales is far too weak to overcome proton repulsion.
- The strong nuclear interaction supplies the short-range binding of nucleons.

## Q21.21: Field ionization

How can a strong electric field remove electrons from atoms, and what sets the necessary field scale?

**Solution:**
The field exerts forces in opposite directions on electrons and the nucleus, distorting the binding potential and allowing escape, including tunneling. An order-of-magnitude scale follows from $eEa\sim E_{\rm bind}$ for atomic size $a$. Actual thresholds depend on the atomic state, binding energy, field duration, and quantum tunneling.

**Why it works:**
External electrical work over an atomic length can compete with the electron's binding energy.

**Grading key points:**

- The field exerts forces in opposite directions on electrons and the nucleus, distorting the binding potential and allowing escape, including tunneling.
- An order-of-magnitude scale follows from $eEa\sim E_{\rm bind}$ for atomic size $a$.
- Actual thresholds depend on the atomic state, binding energy, field duration, and quantum tunneling.

## Q21.23: Scalar and vector fields

Are atmospheric air velocity and air temperature vector fields? Explain.

**Solution:**
Velocity is a vector field because each position has a speed and direction. Temperature is a scalar field because each position has one temperature without a direction.

**Why it works:**
Spatial variation alone does not make a quantity a vector; its values must themselves have direction.

**Grading key points:**

- Velocity is a vector field because each position has a speed and direction.
- Temperature is a scalar field because each position has one temperature without a direction.
