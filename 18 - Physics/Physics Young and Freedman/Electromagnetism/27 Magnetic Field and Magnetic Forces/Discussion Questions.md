## Q27.1: Magnetic-force null directions

Can a charged particle move through a magnetic field without magnetic force?

**Solution:**
Yes. $\mathbf F_B=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ vanishes when velocity is parallel or antiparallel to the field. It also vanishes for zero speed or zero field.

**Why it works:**
Only the velocity component perpendicular to B contributes to magnetic force.

**Grading key points:**

- Yes. $\mathbf F_B=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ vanishes when velocity is parallel or antiparallel to the field.
- It also vanishes for zero speed or zero field.

## Q27.2: Defining magnetic-field direction

Why not define B to point along the magnetic force on a positive moving charge, as E points along electric force on a positive charge?

**Solution:**
Magnetic force is perpendicular to B and also depends on velocity direction. Different moving test charges at the same point can feel different force directions, and a stationary charge feels none. The cross-product relation specifies B consistently.

**Why it works:**
Unlike electric force, magnetic force cannot be determined from charge sign and position alone.

**Grading key points:**

- Magnetic force is perpendicular to B and also depends on velocity direction.
- Different moving test charges at the same point can feel different force directions, and a stationary charge feels none.
- The cross-product relation specifies B consistently.

## Q27.3: Handedness and charge sign

Would using the left hand in the usual right-hand magnetic-force procedure give the correct force?

**Solution:**
For the same positive charge and vectors, it gives the opposite direction and is wrong. That opposite direction is appropriate for a negative charge after the charge-sign reversal is included.

**Why it works:**
The cross product has a fixed right-handed orientation, and multiplying by negative q reverses it.

**Grading key points:**

- For the same positive charge and vectors, it gives the opposite direction and is wrong.
- That opposite direction is appropriate for a negative charge after the charge-sign reversal is included.

## Q27.4: Helical motion

Must a charged particle's trajectory be perpendicular to magnetic field lines because magnetic force is perpendicular to B?

**Solution:**
No. The velocity may have a component parallel to B; that component persists while the perpendicular component rotates, producing a helix in uniform B. Only a particle with zero parallel velocity follows a plane perpendicular to the field.

**Why it works:**
Force direction controls acceleration, not the requirement that all velocity components share that direction.

**Grading key points:**

- No.
- The velocity may have a component parallel to B; that component persists while the perpendicular component rotates, producing a helix in uniform B.
- Only a particle with zero parallel velocity follows a plane perpendicular to the field.

## Q27.5: Magnetic confinement by a finite uniform-field box

Can a particle fired into a finite cube containing uniform B remain trapped there forever by that field alone?

**Solution:**
Not in the ideal force-only model. A nonzero field-parallel velocity eventually carries it to a boundary. With zero parallel velocity, its circular orbit intersects the boundary through which it entered and later exits again. A circle wholly inside could exist for a particle initially placed there, but cannot be reached by entry without energy loss or changing fields.

**Why it works:**
A static uniform magnetic field changes direction without dissipating energy or moving an orbit onto a different circle.

**Grading key points:**

- Not in the ideal force-only model.

## Q27.6: Forces that change direction without work

How can magnetic force affect motion without doing work? Give another example.

**Solution:**
It changes the direction of momentum while remaining perpendicular to velocity, so $P=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v=0$ and speed is unchanged. Tension providing centripetal force in an ideal horizontal circular motion is another example.

**Why it works:**
Work measures change of kinetic energy, not every possible change of velocity.

**Grading key points:**

- It changes the direction of momentum while remaining perpendicular to velocity, so $P=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v=0$ and speed is unchanged.
- Tension providing centripetal force in an ideal horizontal circular motion is another example.

## Q27.7: What constant velocity implies about fields

A charged particle has constant velocity and no nonelectromagnetic force. If B=0, must E=0? If E=0, must B=0?

**Solution:**
If B=0, zero acceleration requires $q\mathbf E=0$, so E=0 for nonzero q. If E=0, B need not vanish; it can be parallel to the nonzero velocity so $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B=0$. A particle at rest also feels no magnetic force for any B.

**Why it works:**
Zero Lorentz force need not imply a zero magnetic field.

**Grading key points:**

- If B=0, zero acceleration requires $q\mathbf E=0$, so E=0 for nonzero q.
- If E=0, B need not vanish; it can be parallel to the nonzero velocity so $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B=0$.
- A particle at rest also feels no magnetic force for any B.

## Q27.8: Current-loop compass

How could a current loop act as a compass, and could it distinguish north from south?

**Solution:**
Suspend it so it can rotate freely. Its magnetic moment $\boldsymbol\mu=IA\hat{\mathbf n}$ tends to align with Earth's field. Knowing the current direction fixes the moment by the right-hand rule and distinguishes opposite field directions. The loop's plane alone, without knowing its normal's sign, would leave an ambiguity.

**Why it works:**
Torque $\boldsymbol\mu\times\mathbf B$ aligns a directed dipole, not an undirected plane.

**Grading key points:**

- Suspend it so it can rotate freely.
- Its magnetic moment $\boldsymbol\mu=IA\hat{\mathbf n}$ tends to align with Earth's field.
- Knowing the current direction fixes the moment by the right-hand rule and distinguishes opposite field directions.
- The loop's plane alone, without knowing its normal's sign, would leave an ambiguity.

## Q27.9: Determining B using wire forces

How can qualitative force observations on a straight current-carrying wire determine a magnetic-field direction?

**Solution:**
Rotate the wire to find orientations with zero magnetic force; these identify the field axis. Then orient it nonparallel to that axis, observe the force direction for a known conventional current direction, and use $\mathbf F=I\mathbf L\times\mathbf B$ to choose the sign along the axis.

**Why it works:**
A zero-force test determines the axis, and a transverse-force test resolves the remaining twofold ambiguity.

**Grading key points:**

- Rotate the wire to find orientations with zero magnetic force; these identify the field axis.
- Then orient it nonparallel to that axis, observe the force direction for a known conventional current direction, and use $\mathbf F=I\mathbf L\times\mathbf B$ to choose the sign along the axis.

## Q27.10: Magnetic force on a flexible loop

A current-carrying floppy loop lies on a horizontal table with vertical uniform B and expands toward a circle. Give the possible current and field orientations.

**Solution:**
Viewed from above, counterclockwise current with B upward gives outward force everywhere; clockwise current with B downward does likewise. Reversing only one makes the force inward. For example, at the rightmost point with counterclockwise current, $d\boldsymbol\ell$ points up the page and B out of it, so $d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$ points right.

**Why it works:**
The two matched orientations produce outward magnetic pressure; a circle maximizes area for a given wire length.

**Grading key points:**

- Viewed from above, counterclockwise current with B upward gives outward force everywhere; clockwise current with B downward does likewise.
- Reversing only one makes the force inward.
- For example, at the rightmost point with counterclockwise current, $d\boldsymbol\ell$ points up the page and B out of it, so $d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$ points right.

## Q27.11: Curvature, speed, and charge sign

Particles enter B directed into the page with velocities in the page. Compare paths for +q at speed v, +q at 2v, -q at v, and a neutral particle, assuming equal masses.

**Solution:**
The positive charges curve counterclockwise, with radii $mv/(|q|B)$ and twice that radius. The negative charge curves clockwise with the first radius. The neutral particle travels straight. For a rightward initial velocity, a positive charge initially deflects upward.

**Why it works:**
Magnetic force supplies centripetal acceleration, so radius grows with momentum and curvature reverses with charge sign.

**Grading key points:**

- The positive charges curve counterclockwise, with radii $mv/(|q|B)$ and twice that radius.
- The negative charge curves clockwise with the first radius.
- The neutral particle travels straight.
- For a rightward initial velocity, a positive charge initially deflects upward.

## Q27.13: Estimating magnetic force on lightning

Could Earth's magnetic field bend a metal flagpole carrying a lightning current of order $10^4$ to $10^5$ A?

**Solution:**
Using a representative $B\sim5\times10^{-5}$ T, the largest force per length is $IB\sim0.5$ to 5 N/m, and less if the pole aligns partly with B. This is generally insufficient to bend a sturdy pole during a brief stroke. An absolute verdict requires pole stiffness, length, field orientation, and pulse duration; heating or other lightning effects can be much larger.

**Why it works:**
The magnetic-force estimate must be compared with structural and impulse scales rather than inferred from the large current alone.

**Grading key points:**

- Using a representative $B\sim5\times10^{-5}$ T, the largest force per length is $IB\sim0.5$ to 5 N/m, and less if the pole aligns partly with B.
- This is generally insufficient to bend a sturdy pole during a brief stroke.
- An absolute verdict requires pole stiffness, length, field orientation, and pulse duration; heating or other lightning effects can be much larger.

## Q27.14: Magnetic steering versus acceleration

Can an accelerator use only magnetic forces both to steer particles and increase their speeds?

**Solution:**
No. Static magnetic forces can bend trajectories but do zero work and cannot increase kinetic energy. Speed increase requires an electric field or another working force; a changing magnetic field can induce the electric field that performs the work.

**Why it works:**
The energy-changing force is electric even when magnetic induction generates it.

**Grading key points:**

- No.
- Static magnetic forces can bend trajectories but do zero work and cannot increase kinetic energy.
- Speed increase requires an electric field or another working force; a changing magnetic field can induce the electric field that performs the work.

## Q27.15: Stray fields and display technology

Why could an ordinary loudspeaker disturb a nearby computer monitor or television, as discussed in the textbook?

**Solution:**
A speaker's permanent magnet and coil field can deflect electron beams and magnetize components in a cathode-ray-tube display, distorting image position or color. This particular beam-deflection explanation applies to CRTs; an LCD or OLED has no scanned electron beam and need not exhibit the same effect.

**Why it works:**
The answer depends on the display mechanism, not just on the word screen.

**Grading key points:**

- A speaker's permanent magnet and coil field can deflect electron beams and magnetize components in a cathode-ray-tube display, distorting image position or color.
- This particular beam-deflection explanation applies to CRTs; an LCD or OLED has no scanned electron beam and need not exhibit the same effect.

## Q27.16: Motor work and magnetic no-work theorem

How can a magnetic torque do mechanical work on a rotating current loop if magnetic force on each charge does no work?

**Solution:**
The charges have both drift and bulk wire-motion velocities. The magnetic force does no work on their total velocity, but transfers energy between drift motion and bulk mechanical motion through the constrained conductor. The source's electric forces maintain current against the induced back emf and supply the net mechanical energy. In a generator the mechanical input supplies electrical energy instead.

**Why it works:**
The no-work theorem for individual magnetic forces is consistent with electromechanical energy transfer in a driven circuit.

**Grading key points:**

- The charges have both drift and bulk wire-motion velocities.
- The magnetic force does no work on their total velocity, but transfers energy between drift motion and bulk mechanical motion through the constrained conductor.
- The source's electric forces maintain current against the induced back emf and supply the net mechanical energy.
- In a generator the mechanical input supplies electrical energy instead.

## Q27.17: Motor as generator

Can a dc motor be used as a generator, and how?

**Solution:**
Yes, for a suitable motor and magnetic excitation. Mechanically drive its shaft so the rotating conductors cut magnetic flux, producing emf at the terminals; a connected load then receives current and electrical power. The opposing electromagnetic torque requires continued mechanical input.

**Why it works:**
Motor and generator operation are reciprocal energy conversions governed by induction and magnetic torque.

## Q27.18: Reversing motor rotation

Why may reversing a dc motor's supply polarity leave its rotation unchanged, and how can rotation be reversed?

**Solution:**
In a wound-field motor where the same supply reversal reverses both armature current and field current, both I and B reverse and torque keeps its sign. Reverse only the armature or only the field connections to reverse torque. In a permanent-magnet motor the field does not reverse, so reversing supply polarity normally reverses rotation.

**Why it works:**
The nonreversal claim depends on motor construction; torque depends on both armature current and field.

**Grading key points:**

- In a wound-field motor where the same supply reversal reverses both armature current and field current, both I and B reverse and torque keeps its sign.
- Reverse only the armature or only the field connections to reverse torque.
- In a permanent-magnet motor the field does not reverse, so reversing supply polarity normally reverses rotation.

## Q27.19: When Hall voltage vanishes

Under what conditions might a Hall experiment show no transverse voltage?

**Solution:**
It vanishes if current or magnetic field is zero, or if the field is parallel to current so the magnetic deflection is zero. In multicarrier materials, electron and hole contributions can also cancel under suitable conditions; incorrect contact geometry may conceal the voltage.

**Why it works:**
Hall voltage requires an uncompensated transverse carrier deflection, not merely the presence of a conductor and B.

**Grading key points:**

- It vanishes if current or magnetic field is zero, or if the field is parallel to current so the magnetic deflection is zero.
- In multicarrier materials, electron and hole contributions can also cancel under suitable conditions; incorrect contact geometry may conceal the voltage.

## Q27.20: Hall voltage and carrier density

Why can germanium give a much larger Hall voltage than copper for comparable current, field, and geometry?

**Solution:**
In the simple single-carrier model, $V_H=IB/(nqt)$, so a smaller mobile-carrier density n gives a larger Hall voltage. Semiconductors can have far fewer mobile carriers than metals. Resistivity alone is not the decisive variable, because mobility also affects resistivity.

**Why it works:**
The Hall field balances magnetic deflection, and the required drift speed at fixed current rises as carrier density falls.

**Grading key points:**

- In the simple single-carrier model, $V_H=IB/(nqt)$, so a smaller mobile-carrier density n gives a larger Hall voltage.
- Semiconductors can have far fewer mobile carriers than metals.
- Resistivity alone is not the decisive variable, because mobility also affects resistivity.
