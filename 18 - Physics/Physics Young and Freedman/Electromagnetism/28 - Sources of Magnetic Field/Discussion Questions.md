## Q28.1: Identifying a monopole

How might an isolated magnetic monopole be recognized, and what properties would distinguish it from an ordinary magnet?

**Solution:**
It would act as a single magnetic pole, with nonzero net magnetic flux through an enclosing surface and field lines beginning or ending on it. It would experience a force along or opposite an applied B according to its magnetic charge. Cutting an ordinary dipole magnet instead gives smaller dipoles, not isolated poles.

**Why it works:**
A monopole would violate the ordinary zero-enclosed-magnetic-charge form of Gauss's law for B.

**Grading key points:**

- It would act as a single magnetic pole, with nonzero net magnetic flux through an enclosing surface and field lines beginning or ending on it.
- It would experience a force along or opposite an applied B according to its magnetic charge.
- Cutting an ordinary dipole magnet instead gives smaller dipoles, not isolated poles.

## Q28.2: Moving charges generate fields

How can solar streams of charged particles disturb Earth's magnetic field?

**Solution:**
Moving charged particles form currents and generate magnetic fields. Their interaction with Earth's magnetosphere drives additional currents, redistributes plasma, and changes the local and global field configuration.

**Why it works:**
The magnetic field responds to moving charge and induced current systems, not only to permanent magnets.

**Grading key points:**

- Moving charged particles form currents and generate magnetic fields.
- Their interaction with Earth's magnetosphere drives additional currents, redistributes plasma, and changes the local and global field configuration.

## Q28.3: Long-wire approximation

How can one decide whether a finite straight current-carrying wire is long enough to use the infinite-wire formula?

**Solution:**
The perpendicular observation distance must be small compared with distances to both ends, and the return path must contribute negligibly or be included separately. Compare with $B=\mu_0I(\sin\theta_1+\sin\theta_2)/(4\pi r)$; the infinite result follows as both angles approach 90 degrees. The allowable error sets how long is long enough.

**Why it works:**
An approximation is controlled by geometry and required accuracy rather than literal infinity.

**Grading key points:**

- The perpendicular observation distance must be small compared with distances to both ends, and the return path must contribute negligibly or be included separately.
- Compare with $B=\mu_0I(\sin\theta_1+\sin\theta_2)/(4\pi r)$; the infinite result follows as both angles approach 90 degrees.

## Q28.4: Work between attracting wires

Parallel wires with same-direction currents attract and can do mechanical work as they approach. Where does the energy come from if magnetic forces on charges do no work?

**Solution:**
With currents maintained by supplies, motion changes flux linkage and induces emf; the supplies and the changing magnetic-field energy account for the mechanical work. If the currents are not maintained, their evolution and field energy must be included instead. Magnetic force transfers energy between microscopic carrier and bulk-motion degrees of freedom without doing work on an individual charge's total velocity.

**Why it works:**
Energy conservation applies to the whole electromechanical system, including its sources and fields.

**Grading key points:**

- With currents maintained by supplies, motion changes flux linkage and induces emf; the supplies and the changing magnetic-field energy account for the mechanical work.
- If the currents are not maintained, their evolution and field energy must be included instead.
- Magnetic force transfers energy between microscopic carrier and bulk-motion degrees of freedom without doing work on an individual charge's total velocity.

## Q28.5: Twisted-pair cancellation

Why can twisting a supply wire together with its return wire reduce unwanted magnetic-field effects?

**Solution:**
Adjacent wires carry nearly equal opposite currents, so their far fields largely cancel. Twisting also makes successive small loop areas alternate orientation, reducing induced pickup and emissions from external changing fields.

**Why it works:**
Small separation reduces the magnetic dipole area and twisting averages spatial coupling.

**Grading key points:**

- Adjacent wires carry nearly equal opposite currents, so their far fields largely cancel.
- Twisting also makes successive small loop areas alternate orientation, reducing induced pickup and emissions from external changing fields.

## Q28.6: Three parallel-wire current signs

Three long parallel wires occupy an equilateral triangle. Can every pair attract? Can every pair repel?

**Solution:**
All pairs attract if all three currents have the same direction. Every pair cannot repel for nonzero currents restricted to the two parallel directions: if wire 1 is opposite to both wires 2 and 3, then wires 2 and 3 share a direction and attract.

**Why it works:**
Pair attraction requires equal current directions, pair repulsion opposite directions; three binary signs cannot all be pairwise opposite.

**Grading key points:**

- All pairs attract if all three currents have the same direction.
- Every pair cannot repel for nonzero currents restricted to the two parallel directions: if wire 1 is opposite to both wires 2 and 3, then wires 2 and 3 share a direction and attract.

## Q28.7: Force from external magnetic field

Why calculate force on one wire using the other wire's field rather than the total including its own field?

**Solution:**
The interaction force is $I\mathbf L\times\mathbf B_{other}$. A wire's self-field does not supply its net external translational force, although it can produce internal stresses. Including an ideal line wire's divergent self-field would also be mathematically invalid.

**Why it works:**
The force between distinct bodies uses the field produced by the other body, with self-stress treated separately.

**Grading key points:**

- The interaction force is $I\mathbf L\times\mathbf B_{other}$.
- A wire's self-field does not supply its net external translational force, although it can produce internal stresses.
- Including an ideal line wire's divergent self-field would also be mathematically invalid.

## Q28.8: Concentric coplanar loops

Two concentric coplanar loops carry currents in the same sense. Describe the magnetic forces on their circumferences.

**Solution:**
The outer loop's field through the inner loop points with their common magnetic moment, producing an outward radial force on the inner loop. Outside the inner loop, its return field has the opposite sign, giving an inward radial force on the outer loop. Each complete loop has zero net translational force by symmetry, though it experiences radial stress.

**Why it works:**
Use local $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$ and distinguish distributed expansion or contraction from a net vector force.

**Grading key points:**

- The outer loop's field through the inner loop points with their common magnetic moment, producing an outward radial force on the inner loop.
- Outside the inner loop, its return field has the opposite sign, giving an inward radial force on the outer loop.
- Each complete loop has zero net translational force by symmetry, though it experiences radial stress.

## Q28.10: Ampere versus Biot-Savart methods

Compare Ampere's law and Biot-Savart for practical magnetic-field calculations.

**Solution:**
For steady currents, Biot-Savart integrates contributions of known current elements and works for general geometry, though integrals may be difficult. Ampere's law is also general in magnetostatics, but directly solves B only when symmetry makes its line integral simple, such as an infinite wire, ideal solenoid, or toroid. Time-dependent situations require the displacement-current extension.

**Why it works:**
An exact integral law is not automatically an easy algebraic field formula without symmetry.

**Grading key points:**

- For steady currents, Biot-Savart integrates contributions of known current elements and works for general geometry, though integrals may be difficult.
- Ampere's law is also general in magnetostatics, but directly solves B only when symmetry makes its line integral simple, such as an infinite wire, ideal solenoid, or toroid.
- Time-dependent situations require the displacement-current extension.

## Q28.11: Closed magnetic field lines

Why can an ideal toroid's field be confined inside, while a finite straight solenoid has an external field?

**Solution:**
A toroid's internal lines can close around its circular core without leaving it. In a finite straight solenoid the internal axial lines must return around the outside to close, giving a nonzero exterior return field. An infinite-solenoid limit can make the external field negligible by placing ends infinitely far away.

**Why it works:**
Gauss's law for magnetism forbids field-line starts and ends; geometry determines the return route.

**Grading key points:**

- A toroid's internal lines can close around its circular core without leaving it.
- In a finite straight solenoid the internal axial lines must return around the outside to close, giving a nonzero exterior return field.
- An infinite-solenoid limit can make the external field negligible by placing ends infinitely far away.

## Q28.13: Cancellation of two-wire fields

Two infinitely long parallel wires carry equal currents. Where does B vanish for opposite current directions, and for the same direction?

**Solution:**
For opposite currents there is no finite null: between the wires their fields reinforce, and outside they oppose but have unequal magnitudes at every finite point. For same-direction equal currents, the midpoint line parallel to the wires is a null: equal magnitudes and opposite directions cancel there.

**Why it works:**
Cancellation requires both opposite field directions and equal distances for equal currents.

**Grading key points:**

- For opposite currents there is no finite null: between the wires their fields reinforce, and outside they oppose but have unequal magnitudes at every finite point.
- For same-direction equal currents, the midpoint line parallel to the wires is a null: equal magnitudes and opposite directions cancel there.

## Q28.15: Loop radius and off-axis distance

A circular current loop gives B0 at its center and B at a point distance x along its axis. At fixed current, what happens if its radius R doubles?

**Solution:**
At the center, $B_0=\mu_0I/(2R)$ becomes $B_0/2$. On the axis, $B(x)=\mu_0IR^2/[2(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}]$, so the ratio is $4[(R^2+x^2)/(4R^2+x^2)]^{3/2}$. It is not generally one-half; far along the axis it approaches 4.

**Why it works:**
Changing radius affects both magnetic moment and source-to-point distance; the center is a special limit.

**Grading key points:**

- At the center, $B_0=\mu_0I/(2R)$ becomes $B_0/2$.
- On the axis, $B(x)=\mu_0IR^2/[2(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}]$, so the ratio is $4[(R^2+x^2)/(4R^2+x^2)]^{3/2}$.
- It is not generally one-half; far along the axis it approaches 4.

## Q28.16: Paramagnetism and temperature

Why does a paramagnet's permeability tend to decrease as temperature increases?

**Solution:**
Permanent microscopic magnetic moments align partially with the applied field, but thermal agitation opposes alignment. In the simple Curie regime $\chi\propto1/T$ and $\mu=\mu_0(1+\chi)$, so permeability approaches the vacuum value as temperature rises.

**Why it works:**
Thermal disorder reduces field-induced orientational order.

**Grading key points:**

- Permanent microscopic magnetic moments align partially with the applied field, but thermal agitation opposes alignment.
- In the simple Curie regime $\chi\propto1/T$ and $\mu=\mu_0(1+\chi)$, so permeability approaches the vacuum value as temperature rises.

## Q28.17: Liquid oxygen attraction

A magnet above liquid air attracts droplets of liquid oxygen but not appreciable nitrogen. What does this say about susceptibility, and why is the effect hard to see in room-temperature air?

**Solution:**
Oxygen is paramagnetic with positive susceptibility; nitrogen has a much smaller diamagnetic response and is not drawn toward stronger fields. Room-temperature oxygen gas still has magnetic attraction, but its low density, greater thermal agitation, mixing, and weak force make visible accumulation negligible compared with cold liquid droplets.

**Why it works:**
Magnetic attraction depends on susceptibility, field gradient, density, and thermal motion; it does not vanish categorically in the gas.

**Grading key points:**

- Oxygen is paramagnetic with positive susceptibility; nitrogen has a much smaller diamagnetic response and is not drawn toward stronger fields.
- Room-temperature oxygen gas still has magnetic attraction, but its low density, greater thermal agitation, mixing, and weak force make visible accumulation negligible compared with cold liquid droplets.

## Q28.18: Atomic magnetic moments

What atomic-structure features tend to distinguish paramagnetic from diamagnetic elements?

**Solution:**
Unpaired electron spin and orbital angular momenta can give permanent atomic magnetic moments and paramagnetism. Filled shells usually pair those moments to near zero, leaving weak induced diamagnetism. Bonding and solid-state collective effects can alter the isolated-atom picture.

**Why it works:**
Permanent unpaired moments can align with a field, while induced diamagnetic moments oppose the applied field.

**Grading key points:**

- Unpaired electron spin and orbital angular momenta can give permanent atomic magnetic moments and paramagnetism.
- Filled shells usually pair those moments to near zero, leaving weak induced diamagnetism.
- Bonding and solid-state collective effects can alter the isolated-atom picture.

## Q28.19: Temperature dependence of magnetic response

Why is paramagnetic susceptibility strongly temperature dependent while ordinary diamagnetic susceptibility is nearly independent of temperature?

**Solution:**
Paramagnetism depends on thermal competition with alignment of permanent moments, giving approximately $1/T$ behavior in a simple regime. Diamagnetism arises mainly from field-induced changes of electronic motion, whose basic response is not set by thermal alignment of permanent moments.

**Why it works:**
Orientational statistics are temperature sensitive; induced orbital response is much less so in ordinary conditions.

**Grading key points:**

- Paramagnetism depends on thermal competition with alignment of permanent moments, giving approximately $1/T$ behavior in a simple regime.
- Diamagnetism arises mainly from field-induced changes of electronic motion, whose basic response is not set by thermal alignment of permanent moments.

## Q28.20: Einstein-de Haas angular momentum

Why can a freely suspended iron cylinder rotate when its axial magnetization reverses?

**Solution:**
Reversing magnetization changes the net angular momentum associated primarily with electron spins and also orbital motion. Through coupling to the lattice, angular momentum is transferred to the body, which rotates to balance the change, accounting for any angular momentum exchanged with the applied-field apparatus.

**Why it works:**
Magnetic moments are linked to angular momentum, and total angular momentum is conserved.

**Grading key points:**

- Reversing magnetization changes the net angular momentum associated primarily with electron spins and also orbital motion.
- Through coupling to the lattice, angular momentum is transferred to the body, which rotates to balance the change, accounting for any angular momentum exchanged with the applied-field apparatus.

## Q28.21: Forces on dipoles in nonuniform fields

Why can magnetized matter feel a net magnetic force if a closed current loop has zero net force in uniform B?

**Solution:**
The zero-force result assumes a uniform field. In a gradient, opposite sides of a microscopic loop experience unequal forces, giving a net dipole force approximately $\nabla(\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B)$ for a fixed moment. Uniform fields can still exert torque.

**Why it works:**
A field gradient creates translational force; a uniform field primarily reorients a dipole.

**Grading key points:**

- The zero-force result assumes a uniform field.
- In a gradient, opposite sides of a microscopic loop experience unequal forces, giving a net dipole force approximately $\nabla(\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B)$ for a fixed moment.
- Uniform fields can still exert torque.

## Q28.22: Magnetic moment units

Show that A m² and J/T are equivalent units for magnetic moment.

**Solution:**
$1\ \mathrm T=1\ \mathrm{N/(A\,m)}$, hence $1\ \mathrm{J/T}=1\ \mathrm{(N\,m)(A\,m/N)}=1\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}$.

**Why it works:**
Both $\mu=IA$ and $U=-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$ must describe the same magnetic moment.

**Grading key points:**

- $1\ \mathrm T=1\ \mathrm{N/(A\,m)}$, hence $1\ \mathrm{J/T}=1\ \mathrm{(N\,m)(A\,m/N)}=1\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}$.
