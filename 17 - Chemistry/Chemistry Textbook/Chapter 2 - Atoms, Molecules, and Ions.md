
# 2.1 - The Early History of Chemistry

Greeks believed that all matter was composed of four fundamental substances: fire, earth, water, and air.  They were the first to consider whether matter was infinitely divisible (continuous) or discrete and composed of small, indivisible particles.  Demokirtos of Abdera and Leucippos coined the term **atomos**, to express this nature.

The next period was dominated by the pseudoscience alchemy, and was concerned with the possibility of transmutation, or turning metals into other metals.  Many alchemists were frauds, but many were also serious scientists.

[[Robert Boyle]], in his book *The Skeptical Chymist* in 1661, and thus the science of Physics and Chemistry were born.  While Boyle also had much to do with formalizing the quantitative behavior of gasses, his other major contribution was his idea that an element in any substance that cannot be broken down into two or more simpler substances.

The discovery or rather recognition of the operation known as combustion resulted in further discovery.  The German chemist [[George Stahl]] postulated that a substance burning in a closed container eventually stopped burning because the air in the container became satured with a substance called phlogiston.  

[[Oxygen]] gas, discovered by [[Joseph Priestley]], an English clergymen and scientist, was found to support vigorous combustion and thus was supposedly low in phlogiston. Originally, Oxygen was actually called dephlogisticated air.

# 2.2 - Fundamental Chemical Laws

By the late eighteenth century, combustion had been studied extensively and many foundational elements had been discovered and continued to grow.

[[Antoine Lavoisier]], a French chemist, was finally able to explain the true nature of combustion.  Lavoisier regarded measurement as the essential operation of chemistry.  His experiments, through careful and deliberate measurement, revealed that mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction, which was formalized in the **law of conservation of mass**, which was the basis for the developments in chemistry in the nineteenth century.

He discovered that it is Oxygen, not phlogiston, which supports combustion, and that life is also supported by a process that involves Oxygen and is similar in many ways to combustion. In 1789, Lavoisier published the first modern chemistry textbook, the *Elementary Treatise on Chemistry*, in which he dictated the unified theories of the time and all those assembled up to that point in time.

Unfortunately, in that same year, he was executed by the people during the French Revolution for his involvement as a tax collector. He was survived by his wife, Marie-Anne Pierette Paulze Lavoisier, who was in many ways a fearsome chemist in her own right and who gathered and protected his research as well as assisting him in many of his foundational experiments.

The Frenchman [[Joseph Proust]] showed that a given compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by mass, better known as the **law of definite proportions**.

This discovery stimulated [[John Dalton]], an English schoolteacher, to think about atoms and their role as the components of elements.  Dalton reasoned that if elements were composed of these tiny particles, and they had a constant ratio, then there should always be the same combination of these atoms in a given compound or element.  This helped explain the law of definite proportions.

Dalton discovered that when two elements form a series of compounds, the ratio of the masses of the second element that combine with $1g$ of the first element are small whole numbers. This is the **law of multiple proportions**.

# 2.3 Dalton's Atomic Theory

In 1808, Dalton published *A New System of Chemical Philosophy*, in which he presented his theory of atoms:

1. Each element is made up of tiny particles called atoms.
2. The atoms of a given element are identical, the atoms of different elements are different in some fundamental way or ways.
3. Chemical compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine with each other. A given compound always has the same relative numbers and types of atoms.
4. Chemical reactions involve reorganization of the atoms--changes in the way they are bound together. The atoms themselves are not changed in a chemical reaction.

Using this among other reasoning, Dalton was the first to compose a table of atomic masses (sometimes called atomic weights). This can be thought of perhaps as the first periodic table of elements.

Many of these masses were later proven to be wrong, but regardless, it was an important step forward in the field of formalizing chemical theory.

The methods to obtain the absolute formulas for compounds were provided by French chemist [[Joseph Gay-Lussac]] and by the hypothesis of an Italian chemist named [[Amadeo Arogadro]].

Avogadro proposed that **at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of different gases contain the same number of particles**. This would mean that the volume of a gas is not determined by their size, but by the number of molecules present.

# 2.4 Early Experiments to Characterize the Atom


## The Electron

The English physicist [[J.J. Thomson]], who studied electrical discharges in partially evacuated tubes called [[Cathode-Ray Tubes]], found that when high voltage was applied to the tube, a "ray" he called a **cathode ray**, since it emanated from the negative electrode, the cathode, was produced. Because it was repelled by the negative pole of an applied electric field, he postulated that the ray was a stream of negatively charged particles called [[Electron|electrons]]. 

Thomson further reasoned that since atoms are electrically neutral, they must be balanced out by some positive charge. In his model of the atom, called the **plum pudding model**, the positive charge was given a positive cloud in which the electrons were embedded, as if raisins in the classic English dish of plum pudding.

## Radioactivity

In the late nineteenth century, scientists discovered that certain elements could produce high-energy radiation. In 1896, French scientist [[Henri Becquerel]] found that a piece of a mineral containing uranium could produce its image on a photographic plate in the absence of light. This he attributed to a spontaneous emission of radiation by the uranium, defined as the the energy released by the decomposition of a nucleus as it forms a different nucleus, thus, **Radioactivity.**

Studies later in the twentieth century led to the discovery of gamma rays, beta particles, and alpha particles. A gamma ray is high-energy Electromagnetic radiation event with no charge; a Beta particle is a high-speed electron; an alpha particle has $2+$ charge, which is twice that of an electron and with a positive sign.  The mass of an alpha particle is 7300 times that of an electron, making it quite heavy.

John Rutherford furthered the concept of the nucleus in the atom by an experiment involving shooting a ray of alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold foil surrounded by a Zinc Sulfide screen.  Expecting for the alpha particles to blast straight through, he was surprised that while some particles did indeed pierce the metal, many were deflected at large angles and some were almost entirely reflected back.

This led to the conclusion that a plum pudding model couldn't be correct, and instead pointed to the idea that a dense center of positive charge rested at the center of the atom, the nucleus.


# 2.5 The Modern View of Atomic Structure


The chemistry of an atom mainly results from its electrons. The nucleus contains protons, which have a positive charge equal to the electron's negative charge which has been determined to a very specific degree, but for simplicity, an electron has a charge of $1-$ and a proton, $1+$. Neutrons have nearly the same mass as a proton but have no charge.  

![[Pasted image 20260511012224.png]]

The nucleus, despite being tiny in relative terms to the overall size of the atom, is a point of intense density, containing nearly all of the mass of the atom (around 99.97%).

The basis for the chemical properties of a given element thus lies in the number and arrangement of its electrons.  This is because the electrons are located within the large volume around the nucleus and are the parts that interact when atoms combine to form molecules.  Thus, the number of electrons possessed by a given atom greatly affects its ability to interact with other atoms.

A [[Sodium]] atom for example has and always has $11$ protons in its nucleus. Since atoms have no net charge, this means it will always have $11$ electrons as well. However, atoms also have neutrons, and different types of the same atom can exist with a different number of neutrons, which are called isotopes.

The **atomic number** of an atom is the amount of protons it has, while the **mass number** is the amount of neutrons and protons it has, due to the fact that the majority of an atom's weight is derived from these two subatomic particles.

Thus, since an isotope has the same atomic number, the way to identify if it is an isotope is to see if the mass number is different.

## Covalent Bonds

The forces that hold atoms together in compounds are called chemical bonds. One way that they can form these bonds is by sharing electrons, which is a covalent bond, the act itself referred to as covalent bonding. The resulting collection of atoms is called a molecule.

The simplest method to represent a molecule or molecules is the chemical formula, which uses the symbols for the elements and subscripts to denote the relative numbers of atoms. For example, the formula for carbon dioxide, $CO_{2}$, shows that each molecule contains $1$ carbon atom and $2$ oxygen atoms.

Examples of molecules that contain covalent bonds are hydrogen, $H_{2}$, water $H_{2}O$, Oxygen $O_{2}$, ammonia $NH_{3}$, and methane $CH_{4}$.

A second type of chemical bond is that which results from attractions among ions. An **ion** is simply an atom or group of atoms (read: **NOT NECESSARILY A MOLECULE**) that has a net positive or negative charge.  When it is a group of ions, it's referred to as a **polyatomic ion**.

When an atom loses an electron, thus possessing a net positive charge, it becomes a **cation**.

## Ionic Solids

Ionic solids are those comprised of oppositely charged ions. They are arranged in alternating fashion, and are brittle when deformed, breaking apart into separate planes of packed ions. These can consist of simple ions, such as in the case of sodium chloride, or of polyatomic ions like in the case of ammonium nitrate, $NH_{4}^+NO_{3}^-$.

Although note that in the case of [[ammonium nitrate]], these polyatomic ions are grouped by an overarching covalent bond.

# 2.7 An Introduction to the Periodic Table

The **Periodic Table of Elements** is a chart that shows all the known elements and gives highlight information on each via description and position.

The numbers shown above each symbol are their atomic number, indicating the number of protons they have. Most elements are [[metals]], which have characteristic physical properties like being efficient conductors of heat and electricity, malleability (they can be hammered into thin sheets), ductility (they can be pulled into wires), and are often lustrous in appearance.

Chemically, metals tend to lose electrons to form positive ions. [[Copper]] is a good example. It is lustrous, though it tarnishes readily, it is an excellent conductor of electricity, and it is readily formed into many shapes.  It is also found in many salts, such as [[Copper Sulfate]], in which Copper is present as $Cu^{2+}$ ions. Copper is a member of the transition metals

## Non-metals

There are relatively few nonmetals, and many of them appear in the upper-right corner of the table, to the right of the heavy line that cascades down and to the right starting at Group 13, except for [[Hydrogen]], which is in the upper-left corner. Chemically, non-metals tend to gain electrons in reactions with metals to form negative ions. Nonmetals often bond to each other by forming covalent bonds. For example, $Cl$ is a typical nonmetal.  Under normal conditions it exists as the polyatomic ion $Cl_{2}$, and it reacts with metals to form salts containing $Cl^-$ ions, like $NaCl$ for example, and it will also form covalent bonds with nonmetals, like in [[Hydrogen Chloride]] gas, $HCl$.


## Groups in the Table

The periodic table is arranged such that elements in the same vertical columns (called groups or families) have similar chemical properties. For example, all of the [[alkali metals]], members of Group 1A, are very active elements that readily form ions with a $1+$ charge when they react with nonmetals.

The members of Group 2A are called the [[alkaline earth metals]], and they form ions with a $2+$ charge when they react with nonmetals. The [[Halogens]], group 7A or 17, all form diatomic molecules, and all but one of them react with metals to form salts containing ions with a $1-$ charge.

The members of group 8A or 18, are known as the [[Noble gases]] and their most recognizable property is their inertness, or small chemical reactivity due to fully filled outer shells of valence electrons. In normal conditions, they are monoatomic gases.

The rows of elements are called **periods**.

# 2.8 - Naming Chemical Compounds

Common names are names sugar of lead, blue vitrol, quicklime, etc.

The first system concerns itself with inorganic **binary compounds**, which are compounds you might have already realized are composed of two elements. This will consider both ionic and covalent compounds.

## Binary Ionic Compounds (Type 1)

These always contain a positive ion (cation) always written first in the formula and then the negative ion (anion). These are the rules:

A monatomic cation has the same as its parent element. So $Na^+$ is just sodium for example.

1. The cation is first, anion is second (as we've said)
2. A monoatomic cation takes its name from the name of the parent element.
3. A monoatomic anion takes its name from the root of the parent element and adding *ide*. So $Cl^-$ would be chloride, instead of chlorine.

And then for the binary compounds themselves, since its always the cation first then the anion, you say it in that order.

So $NaCl$ is Sodium Chloride.
$MgO$ would be Magnesium Oxide

and so on.

## Formulas from Names

When given the systematic name, you can derive the formula as well, given you know the charges of the common ions and thus the ratio of each ion, cation or anion.

Given Calcium Chloride, because $Ca^{2+}$ is the only ion that Calcium forms, and $Cl$ is negative, and typically diatomic, we can say that the formula is $CaCl_{2}$.

## Binary Ionic Compounds (Type II)

Type II binary ionic compounds are those where the metal, typically the cation, can have many types of cations.  In these, the charge on the metal ion must be specified using a Roman Numeral.

You can see this here, with Iron Chloride.

$$
FeCl_{2};FeCl_{3}
$$

Because Iron can have a cation with $2+$ charge, and a cation with $3+$ charge, there must be a different ratio of Chloride to accept it.

So, the first would be called Iron(II) Chloride while the second would be Iron(III) Chloride.

There is also an older system where if a metal formed only two ions, the higher cation would end in *ic* and the lower would end in *ous*. So in the above, Iron(II) would be Ferrous Chloride, and the Iron(III) would be Ferric Chloride.
