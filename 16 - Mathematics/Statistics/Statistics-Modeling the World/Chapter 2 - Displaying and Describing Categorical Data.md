
## The 3 rules of Data Analysis

These are:

**Make a picture**...times three.  Visual demonstration is often the best way to depict data and show patterns within it. However, the way you manipulate and depict it can distort the meaning of the data.

## The Area Principle

This is the idea that the **area occupied by the graph should correspond to the size of the value it represents**.  This is to prevent data from being misleading, such as when a particular data point seems to be significantly higher than others due to its visual depiction, despite it being a moderate or even incredibly small difference in reality.

![[Pasted image 20260116130853.png]]

Consider the following example of 3 restaurants, A, B, and C.  This is how many customers they serve each hour.  By this graph, at first glance, you would be under the impression that restaurant C is wildly successful, and that the other two restaurants need to do everything they can to copy C. However, in reality, they are only marginally behind C, and the visual warping of the data can confound this point.

## Frequency Tables

One of the best ways to organize data when it is categorically ordered is with Frequency tables. These simply demonstrate a count of each type of data point.

![[Pasted image 20260116131141.png]]

A relative frequency table is similar, doing the same type of data collation, but with percentages instead of the raw values.

![[Pasted image 20260116131231.png]]

You simply divide each count by the total value of course and then multiply by 100. So out of 2201 members, 325 were in the **First** category, or 14.77%, and so on.

**Frequency tables describe distribution of categorical variables.** This is because they name the categories and show how frequently they occur, hence the name.

Relative frequency is actually a synonym for percent.


## Bar Charts

A bar chart displays that distribution, and is a natural complement to frequency tables.  However, as we noted above, bar charts can accidentally violate the area principle if we are not careful.  Despite this, they are also quite easy to use to keep true to the area principle, with proper spacing.

A relative frequency bar chart is basically the same as a regular bar chart, just with percentages just like before.

## Pie Chart

Pie charts are good at showing the entire picture at once with good references to each category, and like the name is easily digestible. Good for showing parts of a whole.

## Contingency Table

This is good for showing two categorical variables at the same time, and the distribution as they relate to not only each other but the entire picture at one look.

Each frequency distribution is called a marginal distribution of its respective variable.

![[Pasted image 20260116133933.png]]

For example, in the above the marginal distributions are Survival and Class. If you notice, these values are literally in the margins of the bottom and the right cells.

A **Conditional Distribution** just shows the distribution of one variable for only the individuals who satisfy some condition on another variable.  

In the above, such would be the amount of each **Class** of individuals that survived. So, we know 711 of the 2201 survived. We can further use this to demonstrate the relative frequency of each.

![[Pasted image 20260116134341.png]]

What would be another conditional distribution here?  If Alive was one, then surely Dead would be as well.

![[Pasted image 20260116134434.png]]

Charting these values then becomes relatively simple, and we can use two bar charts of these conditional distributions to see if there are any obvious patterns or correlations.

![[Pasted image 20260116134600.png]]

We can see here that class has an association with the distribution of survival, and that it is different from that of those who perished.  This would mean then that Class and Survival are associated and not independent.

They are independent when the distribution of a single variable is the same across all categories of the other variable.  So, if Survival was similar for all Classes, then we'd consider it Independent, but because it isn't, we don't.

A segmented bar chart is similar to a pie chart in that it shows parts of the whole, the area each part taking up corresponding to its value.
